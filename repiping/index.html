What repiping includes

Repiping replaces the pipes that carry fresh water to your fixtures. A typical whole-house repipe in Burien includes:

  • New hot and cold supply lines to every sink, tub, shower, toilet and appliance
  • New shutoff valves at fixtures, and often a new main shutoff
  • Water heater connections replaced with the new pipe
  • Hose bib and exterior faucet lines
  • Pressure testing and inspection of the new system
  • Optional replacement of the main service line from the meter

Repiping is a major investment, so it's worth comparing estimates from professional plumbing services in Burien before you commit.

Signs your home needs repiping

  • Repeated pinhole leaks in different spots
  • Low water pressure throughout the house
  • Rusty, brown or yellow water, especially after water sits overnight
  • Visible corrosion on exposed pipes in the crawl space or garage
  • Gray plastic polybutylene supply pipe
  • A home old enough to still have original galvanized lines
  • Insurance questions about your pipe type

Why older pipes fail

Galvanized steel rusts from the inside, narrowing the pipe, lowering pressure and eventually leaking. It was common in homes built before the 1960s.

Polybutylene, a gray plastic pipe installed from the late 1970s to the mid-1990s, is prone to failure, and some insurers treat it as a risk.

Older copper can develop pinhole leaks depending on water chemistry and age.

How a repiping job works

  1. AssessmentThe plumber inspects the existing pipe, checks water pressure, counts fixtures and plans new pipe routes.
  2. Estimate and permitYou get a written scope and price. A permit is generally required.
  3. InstallationNew lines run through the crawl space, attic and walls with small openings where needed. Water is usually off only part of each day.
  4. Testing and inspectionThe system is pressure tested and inspected, and fixtures are reconnected.

Ask whether patching and painting wall openings is included or handled by a separate contractor.

Partial or whole-house repiping?

If corrosion is limited to one area, such as the line to the kitchen or the original section of a remodeled house, a partial repipe may be enough. If the whole house has the same aging pipe, replacing it all at once usually costs less overall than fixing one leak after another. A leak detection visit can show how widespread the problem is.

Repiping FAQs

How long does it take to repipe a house?

Many single-family homes in Burien take a few days to repipe, depending on the size of the house, the number of bathrooms and fixtures, and access through the crawl space or attic. Inspection scheduling and wall patching can add time. The plumber can give you a schedule with the estimate.

Can I stay in my home during repiping?

Usually yes. Most plumbers restore water at the end of each workday, so you can keep living in the home. Expect periods without water during the day, some noise, and small openings in walls or ceilings where new pipe is routed. Ask how they protect floors and furniture.

Should I choose PEX or copper for repiping?

Both are approved by code. PEX is flexible, needs fewer fittings, and is usually faster and less expensive to install in existing homes. Copper is rigid, heat tolerant and has a long track record. Ask the plumber to explain the trade-offs for your house and water conditions.

Will repiping improve my water pressure?

If low pressure is caused by corroded galvanized pipe, repiping usually improves it noticeably because the new pipe has its full inside diameter. If the cause is a failing pressure reducing valve, a partly closed main valve, or the supply from the street, those need to be fixed separately.

How do I know if I have galvanized or polybutylene pipe?

Galvanized pipe is dull gray metal with threaded fittings, often visible in the crawl space or near the water heater. Polybutylene is flexible gray plastic pipe, sometimes stamped PB. If you are unsure, a plumber can identify your pipe type during a quick repiping assessment.
